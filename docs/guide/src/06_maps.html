6Maps: from measurements to knowledge

A single laser beam can be wrong: noise, a dropout, a beam grazing a corner. So a robot never trusts one measurement. It keeps an evidence grid, a probabilistic occupancy map in which every measurement adds a little evidence, and it only calls a cell free or blocked once the evidence is clear.

6.1 Log-odds: adding up evidence

For each cell the robot keeps a number L, the log-odds that the cell is occupied: L = ln(p / (1 − p)), where p is the probability of "occupied". L = 0 means 50/50 (no idea), positive means probably occupied, negative probably free. Log-odds are used because independent pieces of evidence can simply be added (the Bayesian update becomes addition). This is the classic occupancy grid of Moravec and Elfes (1985).

A cell is FREE when L ≤ −0.8, OBSTACLE when L ≥ +0.8, and UNKNOWN in between. Example: two laser beams pass through a cell → L = −0.9 → FREE. A cell where one beam ended and two passed: +0.9 − 0.9 = 0 → still UNKNOWN, and the robot waits for more evidence. Cells the camera or bumper proved solid are flagged, and later laser beams can never free them again. That matters for a jacket on the floor: every laser beam flies over it, but the camera saw it.

In simple words

Each cell has a score. Laser beams flying through it nudge the score towards "free", beams stopping in it nudge it towards "blocked". Only when the score passes a threshold does the robot believe it. Its own eyes (the camera) and its own body (the bumper) are trusted more than the laser.

6.2 Two questions, two layers

The map answers "can I drive there?". But a search-and-rescue robot must also answer "has anyone looked there for people?" Only a camera can answer the second question, because the laser cannot recognise a person. So every robot keeps a second layer, searched: the cells a camera (its own or a teammate's) has seen. The laser usually maps much further than the cameras have searched, so the difference is large (grey areas in Figure 6.2).

For exploration the robot uses a combined view (explore_map): a cell counts as FREE or OBSTACLE only if it has also been searched, and as UNKNOWN otherwise. In effect, "unknown" means "still to be searched for people". That includes obstacles only the laser has seen, because a rubble heap the laser bounced off may have a person lying behind or under it.

6.3 Frontiers: the edge of what has been searched

A frontier cell is a searched free cell next to (up, down, left or right) a cell nobody has searched yet. It is the boundary between known and unknown. Driving to a frontier and looking around is guaranteed to reveal something new. This is the idea behind frontier-based exploration (Yamauchi, 1997), the most widely used autonomous exploration method.

Frontier cells are grouped into clusters (connected 8-way, scipy.ndimage.label). A long cluster, say a whole wall of an unexplored hall, is cut into pieces of about 10 cells along its main direction (found with an SVD, the direction in which the cells spread most). Then several robots can work along one big opening. Each piece becomes one possible goal:

  • goal cell: the reachable cell of the piece closest to its centre (the exact centre may be unreachable);
  • value: the number of frontier cells in the piece, a proxy for how much new area it should reveal;
  • distance: the route length from the robot (from its distance field, chapter 8).

6.4 Sharing maps without double counting

Robots that can talk by radio merge their maps every second. Merging must not simply add the evidence: if robot A told robot B about a wall yesterday, and B tells A today, adding would count the same observation twice and make the map over-confident. So the merge keeps, for every cell, the strongest single opinion (the evidence with the largest |L|), and any cell someone proved solid stays an obstacle. The searched layers are merged with a logical OR, and so are the "given up" cells (next section).

6.5 When the sensors cannot resolve a cell

Sometimes a robot stands on a frontier and still cannot tell what is in the cell next to it: a table top blocks the laser, a door frame makes the laser call a passable cell an obstacle, the camera cannot see into a corner. Then the robot probes: it drives slowly into that cell. Either it gets in (the cell is free; a robot knows the floor it stands on) or the bumper stops it (an obstacle, now proven). A cell still unresolved after one probe is given up: it no longer counts as a frontier, and the team shares that decision. This guarantees the search always ends.
