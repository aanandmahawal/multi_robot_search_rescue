1The project in one picture

A building has partly collapsed. Nobody knows its layout any more, or how many people are inside. A small team of ground robots enters through one door. They have to map the building, search every reachable corner with their cameras, recognise the people, and report where they are, without crashing into each other or running out of battery.

RescueThermal AI simulates exactly that mission. It is a complete, working system:

  • a world generator that builds a new damaged building every time (office, apartments, hospital, school, parking garage, warehouse, or an open test ground), with furniture, rubble, hidden victims and objects that look like people;
  • simulated sensors: a spinning laser (LiDAR), a colour camera with depth, and a thermal camera;
  • mapping: each robot builds its own map from noisy measurements, and the team shares maps by radio;
  • a neural network that looks at the camera images and says where a person might be, plus a statistical filter that decides when a sighting is certain enough to report;
  • decision making on three levels: a coverage pattern (how to sweep the space), a team strategy (which robot takes which task) and a route planner (how to drive there);
  • motion with realistic timing, energy use, collision avoidance, dead-end recovery and battery management;
  • a 3-D dashboard in the browser where you configure missions and watch every decision, and scripts that measure how well it all works.
In simple words

Think of four people with torches searching a dark, wrecked house. Each person sketches a floor plan as they walk. They phone each other to share sketches and to split the work ("you take the left wing"). When one of them sees something that might be a person, they walk closer to check before shouting "found someone!". That is what the robots do, with a laser instead of a sketch pad and a neural network instead of eyes.

1.1 The one-second loop

The simulation runs in steps of one second. In every step each robot goes through the same five phases. This loop, sense, detect, communicate, decide, act, is the heartbeat of the whole project, and nearly every later chapter explains one of its boxes.

Two design rules make the simulation honest. First, a robot only uses what its own sensors (or its teammates, over the radio) have told it. It does not know where walls are until its laser hits them, and it does not know where people are until a camera sees them. Second, the ground truth is used for exactly two things: to generate what a sensor would measure, and to score the mission afterwards (was that "person" really a person?).

The next diagram connects every part of the system. Read it from top to bottom. The world produces measurements, measurements become maps and sightings, and three decision layers turn those into motion. Motion changes what the sensors see next.

Interview tip: separate the questions

A strong answer never says "Dijkstra makes the robot intelligent". Each layer answers a different question: coverage: which unsearched area next? strategy: which robot takes it? planner: which route gets there? motion: how does the robot physically follow it? perception: is that a person? Being able to say which layer does what, and why they are separate, is the single most important thing to take from this guide.

1.3 What a mission looks like

A typical mission with 4 robots in the default 28 m × 20 m building takes a few minutes of simulated time. By the end, the whole reachable floor has been searched by a camera, most victims are confirmed, and every robot is back at the base (the blue tent at the entrance, where rescuers wait). The dashboard shows each robot's current goal, its reason in plain words, its planned route and what its planner looked at.
