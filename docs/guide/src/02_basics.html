2Robotics basics you need

Five ideas appear on almost every page of this guide: grids, neighbours, coordinates, maps and the robot's body. This chapter explains each one with the exact numbers the project uses.

2.1 The world as a grid of cells

A computer cannot reason about "the floor" as a continuous surface. So the floor is cut into small squares called cells, like the squares of a chessboard. Every cell is 0.5 m × 0.5 m. The default building is 56 cells wide and 40 cells tall (28 m × 20 m). Each cell is either blocked (a wall, furniture, rubble, a person lying on the floor) or free (a robot can stand there).

Converting between metres and cells is simple arithmetic:

cell = floor(metres / 0.5)     centre = (cell + ½) × 0.5 m

So a point at (3.3 m, 7.9 m) lies in cell (6, 15), whose centre is at (3.25 m, 7.75 m). x grows to the right, y grows downwards (the convention of images and screens), and angles are measured from the +x axis, so 90° points down the map.

A robot occupies exactly one cell at a time. It knows which cell it is in (see the limitation box below), and it moves from a cell to one of its neighbours.

2.2 Neighbours and step costs

From any cell a robot can move to its 8 neighbours: 4 straight (right, left, down, up) and 4 diagonal. A straight step costs 1 (0.5 m of driving) and a diagonal step costs 1.4142 (√2, 0.71 m). One rule matters a lot: no corner cutting. A diagonal move is only allowed when both of the cells it passes between are free. Without that rule a robot could slip "through" the corner where two walls meet, which a real robot of any width cannot do. This one rule is applied identically by every planner in the project, so their routes can be compared fairly.

Where in the code

rescue/mapping.py: MOVES lists the 8 moves and their costs; the corner-cutting test if dx and dy and not (free[y, nx] and free[ny, x]) appears in dijkstra, and the same line is in every planner in rescue/planners.py.

2.3 A 2.5-D world

The 3-D view looks fully three-dimensional, but the simulation stores the world as a heightfield: for every small square of floor, how tall is the thing standing on it? This is called 2.5-D: height is known, but nothing can float above anything else (no tables with empty space underneath). Two grids describe the same world:

GridCell sizeUsed forWhat each cell stores
Navigation grid0.5 m (56 × 40)maps, planning, robot positionsblocked yes/no, tallest height in the cell
Fine grid0.25 m (112 × 80)rendering camera images, LiDAR beams, temperaturesheight (m), colour (RGB), object id, temperature (°C), rubble cover (m)

Heights matter because the sensors see at different heights. The LiDAR's laser sweeps a flat plane 0.40 m above the floor: it sees walls, shelves and cars, but its beams pass over a person lying on the floor (about 0.2–0.3 m tall). The camera sits at 0.55 m, looks 14° down, and anything taller than about 0.60 m blocks its view.

In simple words

The robots live on a flat floor, so they only need to plan in 2-D (x, y and the direction they face). The building still has real heights, because heights decide what each sensor can and cannot see. That is why a 3-D-looking world with a 2-D planner is a sound design for ground robots, not a shortcut.

2.4 Maps: what a robot believes

There are two kinds of "map" and it is essential not to mix them up:

  • The true world: where walls and people really are. Only the simulator knows it.
  • Each robot's own map: what its sensors have told it. Every cell is UNKNOWN (never measured), FREE or OBSTACLE. At the start everything is unknown except the entrance area. This is called an occupancy grid, and chapter 6 explains how measurements fill it in.

A robot plans its routes only through cells its own map calls FREE. If the map is wrong (a laser beam missed a low obstacle), the robot finds out when it drives into it: the bumper stops it, the cell is marked as an obstacle, and it plans again.

2.5 The robot's body and time

The robots are modelled as 25 kg tracked vehicles with differential drive: two tracks, like a tank. Such a robot can turn on the spot by running its tracks in opposite directions, and drives straight when both run at the same speed. In the simulation a move to a neighbouring cell is therefore turn first, then drive, and it takes

t = |Δθ| / ωmax + d / v

where Δθ is the heading change, ωmax = 180°/s is the turning rate, d is 0.5 m (straight) or 0.71 m (diagonal) and v = 0.5 m/s is the driving speed. A straight step takes 1 s, a diagonal 1.41 s, and a 90° turn adds 0.5 s. Every simulated second gives each robot one second of "time credit". A move is started while the credit is positive and its duration is subtracted. So over a whole mission a robot drives exactly at its speed, and sharp turns really cost time.

Limitation: the robots know where they are

Real robots must estimate their own position (localisation) from wheel odometry, an IMU and matching laser scans to the map. Doing that while building the map is called SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping). This project gives every robot its exact cell. It does not simulate SLAM. That keeps the focus on exploration, coordination, perception and planning. Chapter 15 describes what a real deployment would add.

2.6 Random numbers and seeds

Everything random (the building layout, sensor noise, where the victims lie, the random choices inside RRT* and the ant colony) comes from a pseudo-random generator started from a seed. The same seed always gives exactly the same building and, unless you ask the runs to vary, exactly the same mission, move for move. This is what makes fair comparisons possible: to compare A* with Dijkstra, you run both in the same building with the same noise. The project keeps separate random streams for the building (seed), the thermal layer (seed + 7), sensor noise, planning and tie-breaks (run_seed), so changing one never silently changes another.
