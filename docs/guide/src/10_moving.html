10Moving and reacting

A route is a plan; the world does not always agree with it. A laser may have missed a low obstacle, a teammate may stand on the next cell, a corridor may turn out to be a dead end. This chapter explains how the robots cope, one situation at a time.

10.1 Driving a route

Each second a robot receives one second of time credit and follows its route cell by cell. A move costs |Δθ|/180°/s + distance/0.5 m/s (chapter 2), so a robot drives about one straight cell per second. Moves are made in up to 8 small rounds per second, so a fast robot can take several cells per second, and in each round every moving robot states the cell it wants next. Two rules resolve conflicts:

  • No two robots in one cell, and no swapping (two robots passing through each other). If a robot's target is taken or occupied by a robot that is not moving away, it waits this round. Because a waiting robot also blocks whoever wanted its cell, the check is repeated until nothing changes.
  • A robot on its way to check a sighting goes first.

After waiting 3 seconds, a robot drops its route and plans a new one that treats its teammates' cells as blocked: it goes around them.

10.2 Dynamic Window Approach (optional)

With Obstacles = DWA, the route says where to go, but the very next move is chosen locally by the Dynamic Window Approach (Fox, Burgard & Thrun, 1997). The "dynamic window" is the set of moves the robot can make right now: its 8 neighbouring cells that are free on its map, not occupied by a teammate and not a corner cut. Each is scored:

G = 0.45 · heading + 0.25 · clearance + 0.30 · progress − 0.10 · turn
TermMeaningRange
headinghow well the move points at the route two cells ahead: 1 − |angle error|/π0–1
clearancedistance from the new cell to the nearest obstacle or teammate, up to 3 cells (from a Euclidean distance transform)0–1
progresshow much the move shortens the remaining way to the point 4 cells ahead on the route0–1
turnhow sharply the robot must turn (smoothness)0–1

The best move wins. If a teammate stands on the route and every alternative leads backwards, the robot waits instead. Afterwards it rejoins its route from wherever it is, or plans a new one. The effect: robots keep clear of walls and steer around each other instead of queueing. In the measurements, waits for a teammate fell from 14.5 to 3.0 per mission, for about 7 % more mission time.

10.3 What happens when…

10.4 Obstacles in more detail

There are three layers of defence against obstacles, from most to least preferable:

  1. Plan around what is on the map. Routes only use FREE cells, and diagonal moves never cut corners.
  2. Replan before reaching a new obstacle. Every second, a robot checks the next 20 cells of its route against its (just updated) map. The LiDAR sees 8 m ahead, so most obstacles appear on the map long before the robot gets there.
  3. The bumper as the last resort. Things lower than the laser's 40 cm plane and outside the camera's view (a fallen jacket beside the robot) can be missed by both sensors. Driving into one costs a turn and a touch, then the cell is known for good.

A special case is a waypoint of a lawnmower or spiral. Waypoints are planned through unmapped space (free-space assumption). A waypoint that turns out to lie inside an obstacle is skipped, and one that becomes unreachable, even through unknown cells, is skipped too. The robot carries on with the next one.

10.5 Dead ends and loops

Why would a robot loop? Its map changes every second. A goal that looked reachable through a doorway may turn out to be blocked, and replanning can then flip between two routes. The robot therefore remembers its last 24 positions. If, while driving somewhere, it visited at most 4 different cells in that time, or it has been blocked for 8 seconds, it declares a dead end. It then backs off along its own trail to a cell at least 4 cells away (reachable, avoiding teammates), and marks the goal it was heading for as tabu for 60 seconds, so the next decision picks something else. With Dead ends = Off none of this happens, and a robot can stay stuck in a loop. The option exists to show why recovery is needed.

10.6 Revisits

Driving over ground the team has already crossed is not forbidden (it is often the only way out of a room), but with Revisits = Avoid it is discouraged: each cell costs an extra 0.3 cells per earlier team visit, up to 5 visits, in every planner.

cost(route) = Σ step cost + 0.3 · Σ min(visits(cell), 5)

Routes then prefer fresh ground, where the cameras see new things on the way, and robots meet less in busy corridors. Measured: moves over already-driven ground fell from 42 % to 33 %, and waits for teammates from 14.5 to 6.3 per mission, with routes only slightly longer. The Repeat visits layer on the dashboard paints cells redder the more often they were driven through.

Interview tip: "can your robot get stuck?"

"Yes, and the system is built around that fact. Robots replan when the map changes, the bumper catches what the sensors missed, a probe resolves cells the sensors cannot, a loop/stuck detector triggers a back-off with a tabu list, and cells still unresolved after a probe are given up so the search always terminates. With recovery switched off you can watch a robot oscillate, which shows why each layer exists."

Limitation: grid motion

Robots move between cell centres with turn-then-drive timing. There are no wheel dynamics, acceleration limits, slip or continuous velocity commands, and a robot occupies one cell (a point robot on a 0.5 m grid) rather than a body with a real footprint. A real robot would add a velocity controller (e.g. pure pursuit) and inflate obstacles by its radius.
