4Sensors

Every robot carries three sensors that complement each other. The LiDAR measures geometry all around. The RGB-D camera sees colour and distance ahead, including low objects. The thermal camera sees warmth. None of them "knows" anything. Each produces numbers that later stages must interpret.

4.1 LiDAR: a spinning laser

A real LiDAR fires a laser pulse and times the echo: distance = speed of light × round-trip time / 2. In the simulation there is no light to time, so the same result is computed by ray casting: for each of the 240 beam directions, walk along the beam and find the first place where the world is taller than the scan plane.

for every beam angle a (240 of them):
    walk from the robot in steps of 8 cm:  p = robot + t·(cos a, sin a)
    stop at the first sample whose fine-grid height > 0.40 m   (a surface crossing the scan plane)
    refine the last step to 1 cm            (find the surface as precisely as a real laser)
    measured = true distance + Gaussian noise (σ = 2 cm)
    with 1 % probability: the beam returns nothing (dropout)

A beam gives two kinds of information. Every cell it passed through is probably free, because light went through it. The cell where it ended probably contains a surface. Turning beams into this cell-level evidence is called the inverse sensor model. The project counts, for every cell, how many beams passed through it and how many ended in it. A cell is "hit" only if more beams ended in it than passed through it. Two small margins make this robust to 2 cm noise: a beam proves free space only up to 6 cm before its measured end, and its hit is placed 3 cm behind the surface, so a beam that grazes a wall corner doesn't end in the empty cell beyond.

In simple words

The laser is like touching everything around you with 240 very long sticks at waist height, once a second. You learn exactly where walls are, but a person lying on the floor is below your sticks, and a stick cannot tell you whether it touched a box or a human.

4.2 The RGB-D camera

The colour camera is a pinhole camera model: 64 × 48 pixels, 90° field of view, mounted 0.55 m above the floor and tilted 14° down, lit only by the robot's headlamp because the building is dark. Each pixel is rendered by ray marching:

  1. From the camera's focal length f = (W/2) / tan(FOV/2), each pixel (u, v) defines a direction; the tilt rotates it down; the robot's heading rotates it into the world.
  2. March along the ray in 7 cm steps until the ray's height drops below the height of the fine-grid cell it is over (it hit a surface) or below the floor.
  3. The pixel's depth is the distance travelled; its colour is the surface colour × shading (top faces bright, side faces 70 %) × a headlamp fall-off 0.04 + 1.15 / (1 + 0.09 d²); its 3-D point is where the ray stopped. That point is how a detection is later placed on the map.
  4. Sensor imperfections: dust haze grows with distance, exposure varies by −20 %/+15 % per frame, and every pixel gets Gaussian noise.

For the map and for measuring what has been searched, a cheaper geometric version is used (visible_cells): a fan of rays every 1.5° across the 90° field, each stopping at the first cell taller than the camera. Every cell a ray reached counts as "seen by a camera". This defines coverage: the share of reachable floor some camera has looked at.

4.3 The thermal camera

A thermal camera measures infrared radiation and converts it to temperature. In the simulation it is mounted next to the colour camera and uses the same rays. Each pixel reports the temperature of the fine-grid cell its ray hit (chapter 5 explains where those temperatures come from). Then it is made realistic:

  • dust between camera and object pulls the reading 6 % × (1 − e−d/4 m) towards the ambient temperature;
  • soft optics: a Gaussian blur of 0.8 pixel (thermal lenses are blurrier than colour ones);
  • sensor noise of 0.25 °C per pixel, plus a calibration drift of about 0.3 °C shared by the whole frame, as in a real uncooled thermal core.
Limitation: temperatures are simulated

No real heat exists inside a computer. The thermal images are synthetic measurements from a sensor model: each surface has an assigned temperature, and the camera model adds the effects a real camera has (blur, noise, drift, haze). Real thermal cameras also depend on emissivity (shiny metal reads colder than it is), reflections and humidity, which the model does not include.
