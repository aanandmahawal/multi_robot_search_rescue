8.4 Coverage patterns: where next?

A coverage pattern is the robot's plan for sweeping space. The project offers the three classic ones.

Frontier-based exploration (default)

No fixed path. The robot drives to a frontier (the edge of the searched area), makes a 360° camera scan on arrival (four 90° turns), and picks the next frontier. Which frontier is decided by the team strategy (8.5). Frontier exploration adapts to any layout, which is why it suits a building full of walls and doors.

Lawnmower (boustrophedon)

The word means "the way an ox turns when ploughing": straight lanes, alternating direction. The building's rectangle (its outline is known to rescuers; its inside is not) is split into one equal rectangle per robot. For n robots the grid is r × c with r·c = n, choosing the factorisation whose rectangles are the most square. Then each robot is matched to a rectangle so that the total drive to all pattern starts is smallest. That matching is the assignment problem, solved exactly with the Hungarian method (scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment).

Lane spacing. A forward camera with range R and field of view F sees a cell at sideways offset e once the robot is at forward distance f ≥ e (inside the cone) and √(f² + e²) ≤ R. So the band swept while driving has half-width R·sin(F/2). With 25 % overlap between neighbouring lanes:

w = 2 · R · sin(F/2) · (1 − 0.25) = 2 · 5 m · sin 45° · 0.75 ≈ 5.3 m

Lanes run parallel to the rectangle's longer side. That gives the fewest lanes and so the fewest turns, which are the costly part of a sweep (Huang, 2001). With D the width across the lanes:

K = ceil(D / w) lanes,   lane k at a0 + (k + ½) · D / K,   k = 0 … K − 1

So neighbouring lanes are D/K ≤ w apart and the outer lanes lie at most w/2 from the edge: nothing is left between lanes. Even lanes run one way, odd lanes the other, and lane ends stay 1 m from the region's edge.

Spiral (outside in)

From a corner, the robot drives the top edge, then the right edge, the bottom edge and the left edge. Each edge moves inwards by one spacing once it has been driven, until two opposite edges cross (the "spiral matrix" order). The passes lie on the same lines as the lawnmower's, so the same spacing guarantee holds.

From pattern to waypoints to routes

Both patterns start in the region corner nearest to the robot (the pattern is mirrored to put it there). The path is cut into waypoints at most 1.5 m apart. Each waypoint is a sweep goal that the route planner connects to the previous one. So the robot follows the pattern closely, and when an obstacle sits on a lane, the planner routes around it and returns to the lane. A waypoint found inside an obstacle, or unreachable even through unmapped space, is skipped (a red × on the dashboard). Sweep waypoints get no 360° scan, because the lane spacing already guarantees coverage. When a robot's pattern is done, it searches whatever the patterns missed (the shadows behind obstacles) by frontier exploration, so every mode ends only when nothing reachable is left unsearched.

8.5 Team strategies: who goes where?

StrategyHow a goal is chosenStrengthWeakness
randomany reachable goal at randoma baseline to compare againstwanders, overlaps
greedyeach robot takes its nearest goal, ignoring teammates (Yamauchi 1998)simple, no communication neededrobots bunch up on the same frontier
partitionthe building is split into one zone per robot; a robot only works in its own zone, heading there through unexplored spaceno overlap at alla robot in a small zone idles while a big zone is unfinished
coordinatedsequential auction on utility = value − 0.5 × distance, with value cut near teammates' goals (Burgard et al. 2005)spreads the team out, one robot per sightingneeds communication

The auction, step by step

All robots that need a goal take part. Every goal has a value (frontier: its number of cells; sighting: 30 × the current belief that it is a person) and a distance for each robot. Then:

utility(robot, goal) = value − 0.5 × distance_m          (1 m of driving costs half a point)
repeat until every robot has a goal:
    pick the (robot, goal) pair with the highest utility → that robot wins that goal
    for every other goal g, as seen by the remaining robots:
        if g is within 2 m of a won goal:  g is taken (never two robots to one spot)
        if g is within 4 m of a won goal:  value(g) × (distance to it / 4 m)   (a teammate will see most of it)
        a sighting already won by someone: taken (one robot checks each sighting)

Robots whose teammates hold goals from earlier decisions see those goals as already won, so a robot deciding alone still avoids its teammates' targets.

Auction: the best pair overall is R1 → F2 (17). F2 is now taken for R0, whose best remaining goal is F1 (10). The team splits up and both areas are searched at once. A third robot would then see F3's value cut from 8 to 8 × 2.5/4 = 5, because R0 at F1 will already see most of that corner.

Interview tip: "how do coverage, strategy and planning interact with a spiral?"

"The spiral decides where: a list of waypoints 1.5 m apart along shrinking rectangular rings in the robot's own region. Before each waypoint the robot checks for emergencies and for sightings within 6 m. The route planner, whichever one is selected, connects consecutive waypoints on the robot's map, assuming unmapped cells are free and replanning when an obstacle appears. After the spiral, the team strategy takes over for the mop-up. The three layers are independent, so I can swap any one of them and compare fairly."
