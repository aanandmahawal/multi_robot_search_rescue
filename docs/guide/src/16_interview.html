16Interview questions and answers

Seventy-four questions an interviewer could ask about this project, with answers you can give in your own words. Each answer points to the chapter with the details.

The big picture

Describe the project in two sentences.
A simulation of a team of ground robots that search an unknown, damaged building for people using LiDAR, a colour camera and a thermal camera. It covers the full autonomy stack: procedural worlds, sensor models, mapping, a CNN victim detector with a Bayesian confirmation filter, multi-robot task allocation, four route planners, reactive motion and energy management, all measured against ground truth. (Ch. 1)
What is the main loop?
Once per simulated second every robot senses, detects, communicates, decides and acts. It is a closed loop: acting changes what is sensed next, and the map and plans are updated every second. (1.1)
Which parts are "AI"?
Machine learning is used only for perception: a small fully convolutional network turns camera images into a person heatmap. Everything else is classical robotics: occupancy grids, frontier exploration, auctions, graph search, sampling-based planning, swarm optimisation and a Bayesian filter. (7, 8, 9)
What does the robot know, and what does only the simulator know?
The robot knows the building's outline and its own position, and whatever its sensors and teammates tell it. The simulator knows the true layout and where people are, and uses that only to render sensor readings and score results. (1.1)
Is the world 3-D? Why is the planner 2-D?
The world is 2.5-D (a heightfield) and drawn in 3-D. Ground robots move on the floor, so (x, y, heading) is enough for planning. Heights still matter because they decide what the LiDAR (40 cm plane) and camera (0.55 m) can see. Multi-floor or aerial robots would need 3-D maps. (2.3)
How do you make experiments fair?
Seeded random generators with separate streams for building, heat, sensor noise and planning. Changing a setting reruns the same building with the same noise, and "Vary" changes only the mission's random numbers. (2.6)
What was the hardest problem?
Good candidates: making robots with the same evidence reach the same verdict (the order-dependence bug), making the coverage patterns real sweeps instead of goal re-ranking, and the RRT* and ACO corrections found by measuring against a known optimum. (7.6, 8.4, 9.5)
How big is it?
About 9,000 lines: a Python package, a three.js dashboard, experiment scripts and 94 tests. (13)

World and sensors

How are buildings generated?
A shell with one entrance, a layout function per type (rooms along corridors, wards, classrooms, parking bays, racks), random collapse of interior walls, furniture, rubble random walks, then victims, look-alikes and warm objects, and finally a temperature field. Everything comes from one seed. (3.1)
How do you ensure every room is reachable?
Every object is placed tentatively. A flood fill (BFS) from the entrance must show that the reachable area shrank by exactly the object's own cells; otherwise the placement is rejected. (3.1, step 4)
How does your LiDAR work?
Ray casting: 240 beams, 1.5° apart, marched in 8 cm steps (refined to 1 cm) until the fine heightfield exceeds the 40 cm scan plane, up to 8 m. Gaussian range noise of 2 cm and 1 % dropouts are added. Beams passing through a cell are evidence of free space, and beams ending in it evidence of an obstacle. (4.1)
Why can't the LiDAR find people?
A lying person is below its scan plane, so the beams pass over them. Even if a beam hit one, a distance does not say "person". LiDAR maps; cameras find people. (4.1)
How is the camera image produced?
A pinhole model (64 × 48, 90°, tilted 14° down) ray-marched through the heightfield in 7 cm steps: depth, colour × shading × headlamp fall-off, a 3-D point per pixel, then haze, exposure and pixel noise. (4.2)
How is the thermal image produced?
The same rays read the temperature of the surface they hit, then dust attenuation, a 0.8-pixel blur, 0.25 °C pixel noise and a 0.3 °C frame drift are applied. (4.3)
How do you assign temperatures?
Ambient per building minus collapse cooling, a smooth drift field, cooler walls; skin 33–36 °C with possible hypothermia; clothing 4–7 °C cooler; heat through rubble falling as e−d/10 cm; warm look-alikes (dogs 31–36 °C, heaters 42–60 °C, engines 40–65 °C…). (5)
Can the robots find someone under thick rubble?
No, by design: 40–80 cm of rubble lets through under 2 % of the warmth, so no camera sees anything. Those victims are counted separately as undetectable, so the results never pretend otherwise. (5.3)
What is the 40 °C rule?
A physics check: nothing on a living person is hotter than 40 °C, so a "person" detection whose hottest pixel exceeds it is dropped (heaters, engines). It cannot remove dogs, which are in the human range. (5.5)
Is the thermal data real?
No. It is a physically motivated synthetic sensor model. It includes the ambiguities that matter (warm look-alikes, heat blocked by rubble), but not emissivity or reflections. (4.3, 15)

Maps

What is an occupancy grid?
A grid where every cell holds the belief that it is occupied. Here it is log-odds: beams passing through add −0.45, beams ending add +0.9, the laser saturates at ±2.5, and the camera and bumper set ±4. Cells are FREE below −0.8 and OBSTACLE above +0.8. (6.1)
Why log-odds?
Independent evidence combines by addition in log-odds, which is Bayes' rule without multiplications, and it is numerically stable near 0 and 1. (6.1)
What is the difference between "mapped" and "searched"?
Mapped means the robot knows whether it can drive there. Searched means a camera has looked there for people. The laser maps further than cameras search, so exploration is driven by the searched layer. (6.2)
What is a frontier?
A searched free cell next to an unsearched cell: the boundary between known and unknown. Frontier cells are clustered, long clusters are cut into ~10-cell pieces along their main axis (SVD), and each piece becomes a goal with value = its size. (6.3)
How do robots merge maps?
For each cell they keep the strongest single opinion, not the sum, because the same observation passed back and forth must not count twice. Proven-solid cells stay obstacles. Searched layers are ORed. (6.4)
What if the sensors can never resolve a cell?
The robot probes: it drives slowly into the cell. It either gets in or the bumper stops it. If the cell is still unresolved after one probe, it is given up and no longer counts as a frontier, which guarantees termination. (6.5)
Do you do SLAM?
No. Poses are exact, and the project focuses on mapping, exploration and decisions. A real deployment would add localisation, and the decision layers would run unchanged on top. (2.5, 15)

Machine learning and confirmation

What model do you use?
A small fully convolutional CNN (about 73k weights): two conv blocks with pooling, three dilated conv layers, and a 1×1 output. It maps a 48×64 image with 4, 2 or 5 channels to a 12×16 heatmap of "part of a person" per 4×4 block. It is trained from scratch, not YOLO. (7.3)
Where did the training data come from?
The simulator: 200 random disaster zones × 100 views = 20,000 images, biased towards victims and hard negatives, labelled automatically from per-pixel object ids. 40 unseen zones (4,000 images) are used for testing. (7.2)
How did you handle class imbalance?
Person blocks are rare, so the BCE loss up-weights them by √((1−p)/p), capped at √20. Without that the network could win by always predicting "nobody". (7.3)
Why dilated convolutions?
To see a whole 1.75 m body from each output cell without further pooling. Context is what separates a person from a 0.75 m dog. (7.3)
How does a pixel become a map position?
For the peak block of a detection, the nearest depth reading's 3-D point (what an RGB-D camera gives) is the position; with thermal, the block's hottest reading is attached. (7.4)
Why not trust one detection?
Single frames are noisy and fooled by look-alikes. A candidate accumulates log-odds evidence (close looks count 1.0, far ones 0.55, "saw nobody" −0.9) and is confirmed only with evidence ≥ 9, 6 separate frames and one look from ≤ 2.5 m. (7.5)
How were those thresholds chosen?
By a reliability study checked against ground truth, then validated on unseen missions: the stricter rule raised fused precision from 75 % to 89 % for 2 fewer confirmed victims out of 108. (7.5, 14)
You saw a dog confirmed and something else rejected. Is that a bug?
A dog being confirmed is realistic: same temperature as a person, body-like shape. What was a bug is that two robots could reach different verdicts on the same evidence, because of report order and when the verdict was taken. That was fixed: reports are applied in id order and verdicts are taken once per second after sharing. Measured disagreements went from 7 to 0. (7.6)
What is "counted twice"?
A real person confirmed a second time more than 1.6 m from the first report, e.g. head and legs. It is an error in the count, not a phantom, and is reported separately from false alarms. (7.6)
How reliable is it?
With both cameras, 100 of 102 findable victims found and 88 % precision; the colour camera alone misses everyone under thin debris. All numbers come from missions checked against ground truth. (14.2)
What exactly is the input to the network?
One 48 × 64 frame with 4, 2 or 5 channels scaled to 0–1: R, G, B (÷ 255), depth ÷ 7 m, and temperature as (T − 10 °C)/40 °C. The LiDAR is not an input. (7.7)
Why is depth given to every detector?
It gives scale: a small warm blob nearby and a whole body far away can look the same size in pixels. Depth tells the network which it is, and later places the detection on the map. (7.7)
Why a heatmap and not bounding boxes or YOLO?
People lie in any orientation, partly covered, several per frame. A per-block heatmap marks every visible body part, and depth at the peak gives the map position directly. A box detector needs anchors, a larger model and far more data. (7.11)
What is the receptive field, and why does it matter?
How many input pixels can influence one output. With dilations 1, 2 and 4 it grows to 72 px, wider than the 64 px image, so each output block sees the whole body and its surroundings. That is what separates a person from a dog. (7.8)
Explain your loss function.
Binary cross-entropy per 4 × 4 block. Positive blocks are up-weighted by √((1−p)/p), capped at √20, so the rare person blocks are not ignored without making every warm blob a person. (7.9)
What do your precision, recall and AUC mean?
On 4,000 unseen images the fused detector finds 98.3 % of images with a person (recall), 83 % of its flags are real (precision), and it ranks a person image above a non-person image 99.4 % of the time (AUC). The belief filter then lifts reported precision to 88–89 %. (7.9, 14)
How does ML influence where the robots drive?
An uncertain sighting becomes a verify task worth 30 × its belief, which competes with frontiers in the auction. The winner drives over and looks again from a new side, and the new frames go back through the network. (7.0, 8.7)
Where is sensor fusion?
Two places. Early fusion in the network (colour, depth and temperature stacked as channels), and map fusion (LiDAR, depth camera and bumper evidence combined in one occupancy grid, with the closer, more reliable sensors allowed to overrule the laser). (6.1, 7.0)
Is there reinforcement learning, or a learned policy?
No. Every decision is an explicit rule with a printed reason, which I prefer for a safety-critical task: you can always explain why a robot went somewhere. ML is used only where rules fail, in recognising people. (7.11)
Would the detector work on a real robot?
Not as-is: it has only seen simulated images. I would add domain randomisation (textures, lighting, noise), fine-tune on real colour and thermal data, and re-measure precision and recall on real footage. The rest of the pipeline stays the same, as long as the new detector returns the same heatmap. (7.11, 15)

Decisions and coordination

How does a robot choose its next target?
In layers: special goals first (battery, all found, report); then its coverage pattern (next waypoint, or a sighting within 6 m); otherwise the team strategy over frontier and verify goals it can afford; then fallbacks. The chosen goal is handed to the route planner. (8.2)
How are coverage, strategy and planning related?
They answer different questions. The pattern says WHERE next, the strategy WHO takes which task, the planner HOW to get there. Any combination works, so each can be compared fairly. (8.6)
Explain frontier-based exploration.
Repeatedly drive to a boundary between searched and unsearched space and look around. Every trip reveals new area, and the process ends when no reachable frontier remains (Yamauchi 1997). (8.4)
How is the lawnmower's lane spacing chosen?
From the camera footprint: half-width R·sin(F/2), with 25 % overlap, gives w ≈ 5.3 m. Lanes run parallel to the longer side (fewest turns), K = ceil(D/w) lanes at a0 + (k + ½)D/K, so gaps never exceed w. (8.4)
How do multiple robots share a lawnmower sweep?
The building's rectangle is split into an r × c grid of near-square rectangles (r·c = n), and robots are matched to them by the Hungarian method to minimise total travel to the pattern starts. (8.4)
What happens when a waypoint is inside an obstacle?
It is skipped and recorded. The route between waypoints goes around obstacles, and after the pattern, frontier exploration searches what was missed. (8.4)
Explain the auction.
A sequential single-item auction: utility = value − 0.5 × route distance. The best (robot, goal) pair wins, goals within 2 m of it become unavailable and those within 4 m lose value in proportion, and each sighting goes to one robot only. Repeat until every deciding robot has a goal. (8.5)
Why does greedy perform worse?
Every robot takes its nearest frontier, ignoring teammates, so they bunch up on the same opening. In the benchmark greedy needs about 20–25 % longer than partition or the auction to find everyone, and drives more. (8.5, 14.3)
Centralised or distributed?
The auction is computed centrally each second over the robots that are deciding, using goals teammates already hold. A distributed version would exchange bids by radio; the utilities would not change. (15)
How does battery affect allocation?
Tasks are filtered by feasibility: 1.3 × (energy there + 5 s of work) + 1.3 × energy back + 5 % reserve must fit in what is left. Low-charge robots don't bid on far tasks, go home, recharge at 60 W and resume where they stopped. (11)

Route planning

Walk me through Dijkstra.
Priority queue by cost from the start; pop the cheapest, and its cost is final (costs are non-negative); relax its 8 neighbours with costs 1 or √2 and no corner cutting; stop at the goal and follow parent links. O((V+E) log V). (9.2)
Why is Dijkstra the default?
Goal selection needs route distances to many candidates, which one Dijkstra run from the robot provides. With Dijkstra as planner the route to the chosen goal comes free from that same search. (9.2)
What makes A*'s heuristic admissible?
Octile distance is the exact cost on an empty 8-connected grid; obstacles only lengthen routes, so it never overestimates. It is also consistent, which guarantees optimality with a closed set. (9.3)
How much faster is A*?
On 40 random routes: 87 cells expanded against 974, 1.1 ms against 9 ms, with identical route lengths. (9.3)
How does your RRT* work?
Two trees (from start and goal) with RRT* parent choice and rewiring within γ√(log n / n); cost savings are propagated to the subtree; informed sampling inside the ellipse once a route exists; shortcutting; Bresenham rasterisation; A* fallback if invalid. (9.4)
What bug did you find in RRT*?
Tracing lines cell by cell produced staircases that cost up to 18 % more than the octile length. Bresenham fixed it (1.013× optimum). Rewiring also didn't propagate cost savings to descendants; now a test checks cost(child) = cost(parent) + edge for every node. (9.4, 9.5)
Explain your ant colony.
10 ants per round choose cells with probability ∝ ταηβ (α 1, β 4), with η = 1/(1 + detour), where detour = step + h(next) − h(here). Pheromone evaporates 30 % per round, arriving ants deposit Q/L, the best path is reinforced (elitist), and it stops after 3 rounds without improvement. (9.5)
Why is RRT* not better here?
It is built for continuous, high-dimensional spaces. On a small grid with narrow doors, random samples rarely hit the doorway, and grid search is exact and fast. (9.5)

Motion and robustness

What happens when a new obstacle appears?
Every second the next 20 route cells are checked against the updated map. If one is now an obstacle, the robot replans immediately. If the sensors missed it, the bumper marks it as proven solid and the robot replans. (10.3)
How do robots avoid each other?
Each round, robots state their next cell. No two in one cell and no swaps; a blocked robot waits, and after 3 s plans around teammates. With DWA, a local score (heading, clearance, progress, turn) picks a move around them instead. (10.1–10.2)
How do you detect a dead end?
At most 4 different cells in the last 24 positions while driving, or blocked for 8 s. Recovery backs off at least 4 cells along the robot's own trail and puts the goal on a 60 s tabu list. (10.5)
Why not forbid revisits?
Leaving a room often requires driving back through the same corridor. Avoid-revisits is a soft cost (0.3 per earlier visit, up to 5) that prefers fresh ground without making routes impossible. (10.6)
How is time modelled?
Turn then drive: t = |Δθ|/ω + d/v, paid from a per-second time credit, so diagonals take √2 longer and sharp turns cost time. (2.5, 10.1)
How is energy modelled?
Base power (computer 15 W, LiDAR 8 W, cameras 6 W), rolling resistance m·g·Crr/η = 20.4 J/m, copper losses k·v, and turning scrub. About 42 W at 0.5 m/s. (11.1)
Can the mission run forever?
No. Every frontier is eventually searched, probed or given up; breakthrough attempts stop after 4 real obstacles; a robot whose battery can never reach the remaining tasks stays home; and missions stop at 900 s. (6.5, 10.3, 11.2)

Engineering

How do you test a simulation?
With properties that have known answers: planners against Dijkstra's optimum; heuristic admissibility; spacing and ordering of patterns; robots actually driving through every reached waypoint; 100 % coverage for every navigation option; no flat batteries; agreement between robots; reproducibility. 94 tests in total. (13.4)
How do you know your improvements are real?
Measure before and after against ground truth on fixed seeds, then confirm on held-out seeds not used for tuning, as with the confirmation rule. (14)
How does the dashboard get its data?
A threaded Python HTTP server keeps one simulator per browser session. The page requests batches of steps and receives compact JSON (maps as strings and bits), then three.js draws it. (13.3)
What would you do next?
Pose noise and localisation, message-based bidding over a lossy radio, sound as a second victim cue, footprint-aware continuous motion, and domain randomisation to test the detector on real images. (15.2)
