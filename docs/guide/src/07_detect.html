7Finding people: the machine-learning detector

Recognising a person in a noisy, dark, partly buried scene is the one job in this project that simple rules cannot do well. A temperature threshold would flag every dog and puddle of hot water, and a colour rule would flag every red jacket. So the project trains a small convolutional neural network (CNN) on images rendered by the simulator itself, then wraps its output in a statistical filter that only reports a person after repeated, close looks.

7.1 Three detectors, one network

The same network architecture is trained three times, once per sensor set on the dashboard. The only difference is what it is given as input:

7.2 Training data made by the simulator

There is no dataset of labelled images of collapsed buildings with victims. So the simulator makes one. It generates 200 random disaster zones (random building type, damage level, 8–14 victims, 10–19 look-alikes, 4–9 warm objects) and renders 100 camera views in each, 20,000 images in all. The views are deliberately biased towards the interesting cases: about a third aimed at a victim from 0.8–5 m, a third aimed at a look-alike or a warm object (the hard negatives), and a third at random.

Labelling is automatic, because the renderer knows which object every pixel shows. The image is cut into 4 × 4-pixel blocks (a 12 × 16 grid), and a block is labelled "person" when at least 15 % of its pixels show a victim that this sensor could actually see: exposed body parts for the colour detector, and also parts under thin debris (< 20 cm, heat still leaks through) for the thermal and fused detectors. No network is asked to see through concrete. A separate test set of 40 disaster zones never used in training (4,000 images) measures how well each detector generalises.

7.3 The network (VictimNet)

Why this design? A fully convolutional network outputs a heatmap instead of a single yes/no, so one image can contain several people and each gets a position. Batch normalisation keeps training stable. Dilated convolutions widen the receptive field cheaply: dilation 4 on a 12 × 16 map lets a cell take in almost the whole body, which is needed to tell a lying person (1.75 m) from a 0.75 m dog. The whole network has only about 73,000 weights, so it runs on a CPU in milliseconds per frame. It is not YOLO or a pretrained model: it is trained from scratch on the simulator's images.

Training

  • Loss: binary cross-entropy on every block (BCEWithLogitsLoss). Person blocks are rare (a few % of blocks), so they are up-weighted by √((1 − p)/p), capped at √20. Without that, the network could score well by always saying "nobody".
  • Optimiser: AdamW, learning rate 0.002 with cosine decay, weight decay 10−4, batches of 64, 20 epochs.
  • Augmentation: half the batches are mirrored left-right (a person is still a person in a mirror).

7.4 From heatmap to a position on the map

Every other second (every second while scanning or taking a close look) each robot runs its detector on its latest frame:

  1. Blocks with heat ≥ 0.92 are grouped into connected blobs; each blob is one detection with the confidence of its peak block.
  2. Inside the peak block, the nearest depth reading gives the distance, and the renderer's 3-D point for that pixel gives the map position (x, y). This is exactly what a real RGB-D camera provides: pixel + depth → 3-D point.
  3. With a thermal camera, the hottest pixel of the block is attached. If it is above 40 °C, the detection is dropped (a heater or an engine, chapter 5).
  4. For every open candidate that is in clear view within 3 m but produced no detection, a negative report ("I looked there and saw nobody") is sent.

7.5 The belief filter: why one image is never trusted

Reports within 1.3 m of each other are fused into one candidate with an evidence score in log-odds (the same idea as the map in chapter 6):

EventEvidence change
a new candidate starts at−0.6 (most first glimpses are wrong)
a detection with confidence p, seen from ≤ 2.5 m ("close look")+ 1.0 × ln(p / (1 − p))
the same, from further away+ 0.55 × ln(p / (1 − p))
a close look that saw nobody− 0.9 (× 0.55 from further away)

A candidate is CONFIRMED only when all three hold: evidence ≥ 9, seen in ≥ 6 different camera frames, and at least one close look (≤ 2.5 m). It is REJECTED when its evidence falls to −1.5. Uncertain candidates become verify tasks: a robot drives to a spot 0.7–2.2 m from the candidate with a clear line of sight, looks at it for 2 s, and if that was inconclusive, the next look is taken from a different side (a buried person may be visible from one angle only), up to 4 looks.

7.6 One verdict for the whole team, one report per person

Evidence is a plain sum, so the order of reports does not matter. But when a verdict is taken does. The project therefore takes verdicts once per second, after the robots have exchanged reports, and every robot applies new reports in the same order (by report id). Robots that hold the same reports then always hold the same verdicts. (Before this rule, in 6 test missions one robot rejected a spot another had confirmed 7 times; now it is 0.) A confirmation is final; a rejection is not: new, stronger evidence can reopen it.

Finally, a lying body is 1.75 m long and its head and legs can look like two warm shapes. Confirmed sightings closer than 1.6 m are treated as the same person. If they are further apart, the dashboard labels the second one "⧉ counted twice", which is a counting error, not a phantom.

Interview tip: "how does ML fit into the robot?"

"ML does not drive the robot. It turns images into evidence: a heatmap of where a person might be. Classical robotics does the rest: depth turns a pixel into a map position, a Bayesian filter accumulates evidence over many frames and viewpoints, and the decision layer sends a robot for a closer look when the evidence is uncertain. The navigation stack itself is entirely algorithmic."
